Was QWERTY really a mistake we got locked into?

QWERTY is the textbook example of "path dependence": an inferior standard that survives because everyone already uses it. I wanted to know which parts of that story hold up when you actually count.

The debate in one paragraph

In 1985 the economist Paul David told QWERTY's story as a parable: an arrangement built around 1870s typewriter mechanics that the world could no longer escape, even after better layouts like Dvorak appeared. In 1990 Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis replied that the evidence for Dvorak's superiority was weak, so there was no proven inefficiency to be locked into. Later, Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka traced the keyboard's actual development through patents and early typing practice.

What I did

I pulled seven numeric or checkable claims out of those papers. Before running anything, I wrote down what I expected each answer to be. Then I counted them on a corpus of 4,059,288 letters: five public-domain books from Project Gutenberg and twenty Wikipedia articles. To tell "good" from "ordinary", I compared QWERTY and Dvorak against 2,000 randomly shuffled layouts.

My pre-registered predictions hit on 6 of the 7. The miss was Dvorak's hand balance: I expected the quoted figure to be roughly right, and it was 4 points off.

The random baseline changes the story

Against 2,000 random layouts, QWERTY is ordinary on home-row use and hand balance, slightly above average at alternating hands, and better than 95% of random layouts at avoiding same-finger pairs. Whatever its designers intended, it is not a layout built to slow typists down. Dvorak, meanwhile, is an extreme outlier on every measure.

What I concluded

QWERTY is real path dependence. Inefficient lock-in is not demonstrated. Both famous camps get something wrong. David's 70% figure is accurate for letters, not words. The "QWERTY already alternates hands" argument for why Dvorak only wins slightly is contradicted by the counts: Dvorak wins every geometric test by a wide margin.

The open question is the interesting one. If Dvorak wins every geometric measure so clearly, why do the speed studies show such a small gain? Geometry may simply not buy much speed. I haven't tested that yet; nothing here measures typing speed.

Limits

  • These are geometric proxies, not typing speeds.
  • I used a modern 8-finger touch-typing map. Typists in the 1870s and 1880s used other fingerings, so this says nothing about the designers' intent.
  • I didn't model the typebar mechanism, so the anti-jamming story isn't tested here.
  • The corpus is mostly 19th-century prose. Results across the six texts varied by only 1 to 5 points, so genre didn't change any verdict.
